## What is research?

You make decisions every day. Some are small: how to get to work? What to eat for lunch? Others are significant, with consequences that ripple through your life for years. Think back to a recent major decision you faced. Perhaps it was: Which university program should I join? Should I accept this job offer or stay in my current position? Where should my family and I live? Is this healthcare provider the right choice for my elderly parent?

Let's focus on one example that many of you have recently navigated: choosing a graduate program. You likely spent weeks or even months working through this decision. You did not simply pick a program at random or rely solely on a friend's casual recommendation. Instead, you engaged in a deliberate process. You identified programs that matched your interests and career goals. You examined course content, comparing curricula across institutions. You considered practical factors: tuition fees, living costs, location, program duration. You sought out information from current students and alumni about their experiences. You researched career outcomes: where do graduates work? What roles do they fill? What opportunities become available with this qualification? You might have consulted university rankings, though perhaps with some skepticism about what they actually measure. You weighed all this information, considering how different factors mattered to you personally. Some of you may have created spreadsheets, lists of pros and cons, or mental frameworks to organize your thinking.

At the end of this process, you made a choice.

What you did was **research**. You started with a question: "Which program is right for me?" You systematically gathered knowledge to answer that question. You collected different types of information -- factual data about costs and requirements, qualitative insights from people's experiences, comparative information across options. You analyzed what you found, identifying patterns and trade-offs. You evaluated the evidence against your own criteria and circumstances. This is the essence of research: identifying a question or dilemma, and generating the knowledge needed to address it.

This process of inquiry is not unique to program selection. Consider other decisions you or people around you have made recently. A farmer evaluating whether to switch to a new crop variety investigates yields, market prices, water requirements, and seeks advice from extension officers and neighboring farmers. A community leader deciding whether to support a new infrastructure project gathers information about costs, benefits, potential disruptions, and residents' opinions. A health worker choosing between treatment protocols reviews and follows medical guidelines, considers resource availability, and reflects on patient outcomes from past cases. A civil servant drafting policy recommendations examines existing legislation, reviews international examples, analyzes data on the problem's scope, and consults affected stakeholders. In each case, someone faces a question and works systematically to generate the knowledge needed to answer it.

You already know how to do research. You have been doing it throughout your life, often without labeling it as such. You know how to identify what you need to know. You know how to find information from multiple sources. You know how to compare, analyze, and weigh evidence. You know how to make judgments based on what you have learned. These are fundamental research skills, and you bring them to this course.

What this book will teach you is how to conduct research more **systematically**, more **rigorously**, and more **transparently**. Systematic research follows clear procedures and treats all cases consistently, rather than cherry-picking convenient information. Rigorous research is carefully planned and executed, with attention to quality and potential biases at every step. Transparent research documents its decisions and processes clearly enough that others can understand, evaluate, and even reproduce what you have done. These characteristics distinguish casual inquiry from credible research, and they are particularly essential when research will inform policy decisions that affect people's lives.

The difference between your program selection process and the research you will learn here is not one of kind but of degree. When choosing a program, you probably did not write down your exact search methods, document every source consulted, or explicitly state your selection criteria in a way that someone else could replicate your process. You might not have systematically considered whether the student testimonials you read were representative or biased. You likely did not test your assumptions about what makes a "good" program. These are reasonable shortcuts for a personal decision. But when research will inform public policy -- when it will shape how governments allocate resources, design interventions, or regulate behavior -- such rigor becomes not optional but essential.

This chapter will help you understand what transforms everyday inquiry into credible research. We will explore what research is and why it matters for policy. We will examine what makes research scientific and trustworthy. We will look at different types and approaches to research, and how they connect to different policy needs. We will provide tools for formulating strong research questions and assessing research quality. Throughout, we will be honest about the challenges and limitations of research in the social world, while showing why it remains our best tool for improving policy decisions.

The journey from casual inquiry to systematic research is not about learning entirely new ways of thinking. It is about building on skills you already possess, sharpening them, and learning to apply them with the rigor and transparency that policy research requires.
