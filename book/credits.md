## How the book is made
This website is written in MarkDown and Jupyter Notebooks files, which are converted to HTML using tools from [TeachBooks](https://teachbooks.io/). The files are stored on a [public GitHub repository](https://github.com/MaastrichtUniversityPress/methods-policy-analysis/). The website can be viewed at `<link to book website url>`.

To recreate the website, you have two options:
- In the GitHub interface: fork this repository, enable GitHub Pages from the source GitHub actions (Settings -- Code and automation -- Pages -- Build and deployment -- Source -- GitHub Actions), enable workflows (Actions -- I understand my workflows, go ahead and enable them) and run the call-deploy-book workflow (Actions -- call-deploy-book -- Run workflow -- Run workflow). The website is released on the URL as shown on the workflow summary when the workflow has finished (Actions - call-deploy-book - call-deploy-book - Summary).
- On your own computer: clone this repository, install the required packages (`pip install -r requirements.txt`) and build the book (`teachbooks build book`). The website is stored locally in `book/_build/index.html`.

More information can be found in the [TeachBooks manual](https://teachbooks.io/manual/).

## About the Editors

**Lutz F. Krebs** is a senior lecturer and political scientist with a focus on international relations and conflict research. Between 2017 and 2023, Krebs contributed to the development of the new, strongly interdisciplinary B.Sc. in Global Studies offered collaboratively by all faculties of Maastricht University. He took a leading role in the successful macro-efficiency application in 2018 and initial accreditation in 2019, and served as founding program director for four years. Between 2010 and 2018, Krebs served as the education director for the M.Sc. program in Public Policy and Human Development. He led the conversion of this program into one of the first master's degrees offered by the United Nations University, contributed to the launch of several specialization tracks, and led the effort for reaccreditation as a joint degree with Maastricht University. Having completed graduate degrees in economics, political science, and humanitarian assistance, Krebs’ studies focus on the impact of democratization processes on the likelihood of civil-war onset in ethnically heterogeneous countries as part of the Swiss National Center of Competence in Research "Challenges to Democracy in the 21st Century". He collaborated in the development of a new coding method for democratization periods and contributed to the launch of the peer-reviewed journal Living Reviews in Democracy. For his dissertation at ETH Zurich, Krebs investigated the influence of political leaders on the likelihood of civil-war onset during democratization phases, challenging the theory of "elite manipulation" on the grounds that the theorized extensive influence of political leaders is unrealistic. Krebs also published an edited volume of student studies on the functioning of actors in the global civil society.

### Statement on the use of generative AI

This book was initially developed over the course of 2025 and 2026, at a time when the capabilities of large-language models made incredible leaps forward. We feel that generative AI can be a substantial help in many professional tasks. We are also weary of the unique issues that result from using non-deterministic systems whose inner workings cannot be examined for scientific work that is founded on transparency and replicability.

For this reason, it is essential to be clear about the ways in which generative AI models have been used in the development of this book. We have considered both the general nature of LLMs and the likelihood with which the respective knowledge is contained in the training datasets 

1. Identifying relevant cases and literature: given our goal to be accessible and inclusive for audiences outside of the Western world or Global North, having access to a “research assistant” that can process many more languages and can identify interesting cases has been invaluable. As far as we can tell, the training corpuses of leading models show a substantial bias towards high-income countries, but they are nonetheless much more inclusive than an individual researcher or a small group of scholars could possibly be.
2. Reviewing, summarizing and critiquing the material we have prepared for the curriculum: this book represents a format shift from a lived curriculum. Maastricht University prides itself in constructive, collaborative, contextual and self-directed (CCCS) learning, which expands upon the problem-based learning (PBL) approach but keeps its two core tenets: small groups with guidance by a tutor, and focusing learning on a problem or issue. In such a classroom, it is remarkably easy to contextualize information, make links to preceding and succeeding content units, and help students connect new concepts to existing knowledge. In a book, this needs to be done explicitly and mindfully, without having the benefit of ongoing discussion with the audience. 
3. Editing for structure and language: we are using AI tools such as [LanguageTool.org](https://languagetool.org/) for continuous editorial advice on our writing for two specific reasons. Firstly, most of us are non-native English speakers ourselves. Secondly, our target audience consists predominantly of non-native English speakers. It is doubly important that we focus on keeping our writing, clear, simple and accessible. Thankfully, the training corpuses of LLMs contain substantial discussions on language, structure and word choice and we've found feedback and advice from a generative AI model helpful on several occasions. It should be noted that LLMs do not provide writing advice of the same quality as a human editor, but they are constantly and immediately available and can therefore be helpful at a much more fine-grained level.
4. Code translation and expansion: the book aims to provide documentation for two statistical packages commonly used in policy analysis: STATA and R. To date, LLMs do not perform well in writing STATA code, likely due to an absence or under-emphasis of STATA code in their training datasets. We have tested this repeatedly and have observed aberrant behavior including switching to other languages mid-code, misunderstanding the grammar of STATA, and a lack of awareness of changes over time. As such, we have refrained from having LLMs author any STATA code. However, generative AI models have been helpful in ensuring that STATA and R code is aligned, contributing to translations of code from STATA to R and expanding R code as needed. R, as a key data science language, is well-represented in training corpuses and the resulting code is of high quality.

As per the current version, the following LLMs have been used:
- Anthropic’s [Claude](https://claude.ai) Sonnet and Opus, up to version 5.0, as primary research assistant. At the time of writing, Claude is among the leading models and Anthropic has taken explicit (though early and limited) steps to ensure that rights holders are compensated for material used in training datasets.
- [LanguageTool.org](https://languagetool.org/), a long-running open-source project released by a team of linguists and machine-learning experts since 2003, for support in fine-tuning our language.
- Alibaba’s [Qwen] for coding work. Qwen is well-trained for coding tasks and features a large context window. It outputs high-quality R code. We're using Qwen up to version 3.8. parameters.
