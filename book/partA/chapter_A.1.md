# Understanding Policy Research

### The evening of November 8, 2016

Across India, 1.3 billion people watched or heard an extraordinary announcement. Prime Minister Narendra Modi declared that effective at midnight -- in just four hours -- the 500 and 1,000 rupee notes that made up 86% of all currency in circulation would no longer be legal tender. Citizens would have until the end of the year to exchange their old notes for new ones at banks.

The stated goal was bold and appealing: eliminate "black money" -- untaxed wealth hidden in cash. The government promised this single stroke would destroy counterfeit currency, dismantle corruption, formalize the informal economy, and expand the tax base. On paper, it sounded decisive. In practice, it became what the Harvard Business Review would later call "case study in poor policy and even poorer execution" {cite:p}`hbr2017lessons`.

Long before midnight, queues had formed at ATMs and petrol stations across the country. The machines ran out of cash within hours. The consequences of the policy had begun to assert themselves

### The human cost

In the days and weeks that followed, the impacts rippled across India:

**Surinder Sindhu**, a farmer in Rohtak, Haryana, recalled the immediate crisis. "It became a nightmare as we had no cash to buy seeds for sowing crops and my daughter's wedding was weeks away. There was no money in the banks, poor people suffered the most," he later told Al Jazeera {cite:p}`aljazeera2018blackmoney`.

His experience was far from unique. Farmers across India found themselves unable to purchase seeds during the critical sowing season. Some could not sell their produce because customers had no cash. As one Al Jazeera report documented, farmers "were unable to even recover the cost of transportation, let alone make a profit" {cite:p}`aljazeera2017oneyear`.

The construction industry nearly ground to a halt. Chotelal Rajput, a contractor, watched his business collapse: "GST and demonetisation have really depressed the construction industry," he told reporters {cite:p}`aljazeera2018discontent`. In Ludhiana alone, over 70% of the city's 12,000 hosiery factories shut down, eliminating jobs for more than 400,000 workers. Across India, an estimated 1.5 million jobs disappeared in the first four months of 2017 {cite:p}`timesnow2017demonetisationeffect`.

For daily wage workers in India's vast informal sector -- which employs about 80% of the workforce {cite:p}`ilo2024indiaprofile` -- the cash shortage was catastrophic. One family reported that they "slept without food for days" {cite:p}`aljazeera2017hardship`. Workers who showed up ready to labor could not be paid. Small vendors watched produce rot because customers had no money to buy anything.

And then there were the deaths.

**Vishwanath Vartak**, 73 years old, collapsed and died while waiting in a queue at a State Bank of India branch in Mumbai. **Mujammil Khan**, a 39-year-old autorickshaw driver from Uttar Pradesh, died in a queue outside an ATM, leaving behind his wife and four children {cite:p}`ibrahim2017financial`. **Sukhdev Singh**, 50, suffered a cardiac arrest in Punjab after he could not use his demonetised notes to pay for his daughter's wedding {cite:p}`aljazeera2017hardship`.

On December 8, a 50-year-old mother died while standing in an ATM queue, trying to withdraw money for her own daughter's wedding. Her son later told reporters: "My mother died because of demonetisation" {cite:p}`aljazeera2017hardship`.

The youngest victim was **Komali**, just 18 months old, who died because her parents had no money to buy medicine {cite:p}`aljazeera2017hardship`.

Different news organizations documented between 82 and 105 deaths connected to the demonetisation policy {cite:p}`catchnews2016deaths,ibrahim2017financial`. The government acknowledged only four. The deaths fell into four categories: people who died or collapsed while waiting in queues, those who died from stress of being unable to find currency for urgent needs like medical care, those who committed suicide out of frustration, and bank employees who died from overwork {cite:p}`ibrahim2017financial`.

### What the data showed

When researchers later examined the impacts, the numbers confirmed what millions had experienced. Economic output, employment, and bank credit each fell by approximately two percentage points during the critical months following the announcement. Overall economic activity declined by at least three percentage points in November and December 2016 alone. Sales across the economy dropped roughly 20% on average as the cash shortage rippled through markets and supply chains {cite:p}`chodorow-reich2020cash,nber2019india`.

The effects were particularly severe for India's most vulnerable populations. Rural poor households experienced economic losses averaging 15.5% over the two months following the announcement {cite:p}`aggarwal2020short`. Farming households faced severe difficulty both procuring necessary inputs like seeds and fertilizer, and selling their produce in markets where buyers had no cash. Daily laborers, when they could find work at all, often received their wages paid not in currency but as IOUs (“I Owe You”), promises to pay later when cash became available {cite:p}`aggarwal2020short`.

And the policy's core objective? Over 99% of the demonetized currency eventually returned to banks through legitimate channels. The vast hoards of black money that were supposed to vanish from the economy simply did not materialize. As researchers concluded in the American Economic Association's Journal of Economic Perspectives, "evidence over the subsequent three years suggests the move had limited success in achieving its stated objectives" {cite:p}`crouzet2020demonetization`, confirming assessments in the international media weeks after the experiment, noting that the policy was "atrociously planned and executed" {cite:p}`nyteds2017costofcrisis`.

## What was missing

Looking back, what went wrong? The policy was not implemented by incompetent officials. Prime Minister Modi and his advisors were intelligent, educated individuals. The stated goals -- reducing corruption, expanding the tax base, formalizing the economy -- were legitimate policy objectives that many economists would support. So why did a well-intentioned policy create such widespread disruption while failing to achieve its aims?

The answer lies not in the goals but in the process -- specifically, the lack of dilligence in preparing the policy for implementation.

There was no pilot program to test the approach on a smaller scale. No gradual phase-in to allow the economy to adjust. No public consultation about implementation challenges. Economist Prabhat Patnaik told Al Jazeera: "It seems utterly ridiculous that no measures were taken to provide currency notes to millions of people across the country before such a big step was taken" {cite:p}`aljazeera2017hardship`.

Most critically, there was limited evidence that such a shock approach would actually achieve the stated objectives. The questions were predictable, and the answers were available to anyone who asked them systematically. How do you replace 86% of physical currency when printing capacity is limited? What happens to daily wage workers who cannot access banks? How do rural areas with limited banking infrastructure cope? What occurs in a largely cash-based economy when cash suddenly disappears? What historical evidence exists about the effectiveness of sudden currency demonetizations? Who will be most vulnerable, and how can harm be mitigated?

These were not unanswerable questions. They required research: systematic inquiry into how economies function, how cash circulates, who depends on it, what alternatives exist, and what evidence from other contexts might inform policy design. The distinction between what policymakers hoped would happen and what evidence suggested would happen proved crucial. Intentions matter, but they are not enough.

## A different approach

Consider an alternative scenario. Imagine that before implementing such a dramatic policy, the government had commissioned research on cash usage patterns across different sectors and regions to understand who depends on cash and why. Imagine they had conducted pilot tests in one state, carefully collecting data on impacts and adjusting the approach accordingly before rolling it out nationally. They could have surveyed vulnerable populations about their banking access and cash dependencies, identifying in advance who would struggle most and why. Economic modeling could have assessed the logistics of currency replacement given actual printing capacity and distribution networks, revealing whether the planned timeline was feasible. Consultations with economists could have surfaced potential unintended consequences and mitigation strategies. Examining evidence from other countries that had reformed their currency systems could have provided lessons about what works and what fails.

With such preparation, the government could have developed support systems for predictable negative impacts on vulnerable populations before those impacts occurred. They could have phased implementation to allow time for learning and adjustment, rather than attempting everything at once. They could have established monitoring systems to track effects in real time and respond to emerging problems as they arose, rather than discovering them only after the damage was done.

Would this have guaranteed success? No. Policy-making in complex social and economic systems never comes with guarantees. Unexpected consequences always emerge. But it would have been evidence-based policy. It would have replaced hopeful assumptions with systematic inquiry. It would have anticipated foreseeable problems -- like Surinder Sindhu's inability to buy seeds, or the deaths of elderly people in bank queues -- and prepared responses. It would have balanced the urgency of policy goals against the reality of implementation constraints and human vulnerabilities.

## Why this matters for you

This is a book about research methods for policy analysis. It might seem strange to begin with a policy failure rather than a success, with disruption rather than achievement, with deaths in queues rather than triumph. But this story illustrates precisely why the skills you will learn in this book matter.

Behind every policy decision are real people. People like Surinder Sindhu, trying to plant crops and marry off his daughter. Like the 50-year-old mother who died seeking money for her own daughter's wedding. Like Mujammil Khan, the autorickshaw driver who left behind four children. Like the families who went to bed hungry because the cash economy had frozen. Like the hundreds of thousands of workers who lost their livelihoods.

Their lives were shaped by whether policymakers based decisions on evidence or assumptions, on systematic research or hopeful guesses, on rigorous analysis or political intuition. Research is not an academic exercise disconnected from human welfare. It is the difference between policies that work and policies that fail, between achieving goals and creating unintended harm, between learning from experience and repeating mistakes, between foreseeing problems and stumbling into catastrophe.

You are studying to become policy professionals -- people who will commission research, interpret findings, design interventions, and make recommendations that affect others' lives. Perhaps you will work in a government ministry, an international development organization, a research institute, a non-governmental organization, or a consulting firm. Wherever you work, you will encounter situations where evidence is incomplete, time is short, political pressure is intense, and decisions must be made. The question this book addresses is not whether to use research in policy -- the India demonetization case makes that answer clear -- but how to do research well, how to assess research critically, and how to use evidence appropriately in the messy reality of policy-making.

India's demonetization offers important lessons. Good intentions are essential but insufficient. Bold action is sometimes necessary but rarely sufficient on its own. Technical expertise matters but is not enough. What transforms intentions, boldness, and expertise into effective policy is evidence: systematic knowledge about how the world works, what effects interventions actually produce, who is affected and how, what works in which contexts and why.

Research helps us ask the right questions before we commit to irreversible actions. It helps us understand who will be affected by our policies and how. It helps us anticipate problems we can solve and prepare for problems we cannot prevent. It helps us learn from the experiences of others and from our own past efforts. It helps us distinguish wishful thinking from realistic expectations. It does not eliminate uncertainty or guarantee success, but it improves our odds and helps us avoid the most catastrophic failures.

The good news -- and this is the foundation on which this entire book rests -- is that research skills can be learned. You do not need to be a mathematical genius or a born researcher. You need to be systematic, rigorous, and honest: about what you know, what you do not know, what the evidence actually shows, and where uncertainty remains. You need to ask good questions, gather relevant information, analyze it carefully, acknowledge its limitations, and communicate findings clearly. You already do these things in your daily life when you make important decisions. This book will teach you to do them well enough to improve policy decisions that affect people's lives.

The stakes are real. Surinder Sindhu and millions like him deserve policies based on evidence, not assumptions. The 82 to 105 people who died in bank queues deserved better. The families who went hungry, the workers who lost jobs, the farmers who could not plant crops, the parents who could not afford medicine for sick children -- all deserved policies informed by systematic research into predictable consequences and vulnerable populations.

The need is urgent. Let us equip ourselves with the necessary skills.
